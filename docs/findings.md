# Key findings

What the Education City student data says, from application to a year after graduation. **Data as of:** the 60 files to academic year 2024–25, built and checked on 5 November 2026. Every number below can be found on the dashboard (https://qfstudentjourney.streamlit.app) in the view given under **See it**.

**How to read the numbers.** Universities aren't in the data every year, so totals for "all universities that reported" can jump for reasons that have nothing to do with students. Comparisons over time therefore use **the same universities in every year** (the dashboard's *Same every year* setting, D-036); the set is named each time. Single-year numbers are for every university that reported.

## The student journey in eleven findings

### 1. Education City nearly doubled in size, and became more Qatari
- The five universities with data in every year since 2011–12 (GU-Q, NU-Q, TAMU-Q, WCM-Q, HEC-Q) went from **1,132 students in 2011–12 to 2,163 in 2024–25: up 91%**.
- Their Qatari share rose from **34% to about 47% by 2015–16**, and has stayed at 46–48% since.
- Their female share has been **about 60% since 2017–18** (58–61%), up from 52% in 2014–15.
- All universities that reported: **2,940 students in 2024–25**, 47% Qatari and 62% female.

**See it:** 1 · Growth, *Same every year*. **Keep in mind:** before 2019–20 the numbers come from QF's consolidated file (D-029); gender isn't available for 2011–14 and 2015–16.

### 2. Getting in has become much more competitive
- At the four universities with admissions data every year (GU-Q, NU-Q, WCM-Q, HEC-Q), from 2019–20 to 2024–25: **applicants up 139%** (1,825 → 4,361), **admitted up 80%** (743 → 1,340), **new students up 61%** (432 → 694).
- Their acceptance rate fell from **41% to 31%**.
- CMU-Q is the extreme: applicants rose from 785 to 2,886 and its acceptance rate fell from **28% to 7%**. GU-Q's fell from 45% to 18%. HEC-Q, mostly executive programs, admits 85%.

**See it:** 2 · Getting in, *Same every year*; choose CMU-Q or GU-Q for single universities. **Keep in mind:** HBKU reports far more applicants than anyone else (21,252 in 2023–24), so Education City's acceptance rate depends on whether it's included; TAMU-Q's 2024–25 admissions are left out (a Spring file covering one program, D-038).

### 3. Applicants are increasingly international; Qatari applicants are more likely to enrol
- At the same four universities, the Qatari share of **applicants** fell from **34% to 21%** (2019–20 → 2024–25).
- At every stage the Qatari share rises: in 2023–24, **24% of applicants, 34% of those admitted and 42% of new students** were Qatari.
- The Qatari share of new students was 42–44% from 2019–20 to 2023–24, then fell to 33% in 2024–25, a single year worth watching.

**See it:** 2 · Getting in, *Qatari students at each stage*, *Same every year*.

### 4. International-school applicants are many; private-school students are the largest group who enrol
- In 2024–25, **72% of undergraduate and foundation applicants** came from international schools, but they were **35% of new students**.
- Private schools: **18% of applicants, 42% of new students**. QF schools: **4% of applicants, 11% of new students**.
- At the three universities with this data every year (GU-Q, NU-Q, WCM-Q), international schools' share of applicants rose from 54% (2021–22) to 70% (2024–25).

**See it:** 2 · Getting in, *Which schools students come from*, 2024–25. **Keep in mind:** from the Student Pipeline sheet, which doesn't cover every student at every university (marked partial).

### 5. Students come from about 100 countries
- **95 countries in 2024–25** (between 83 and 107 a year since 2019–20).
- 2024–25: **45% from Qatar**, 13% from other Arab countries, 8% from South Asia, 6% from North America.
- At the four universities with nationality data every year (GU-Q, NU-Q, TAMU-Q, WCM-Q), the share from Qatar rose from **45% to 51%** (2019–20 → 2024–25).

**See it:** 3 · Studying, *Where students come from*. **Keep in mind:** 6–11% of students' nationality isn't given in a year; countries with fewer than 5 students are counted but not named.

### 6. Government sponsorship is the most common way students pay
- 2024–25, all levels: **38% government-sponsored, 23% self-funded, 19% on financial aid**, 6% company-sponsored.

**See it:** 3 · Studying, *How students pay*. **Keep in mind:** a student can have more than one type, so these don't add up to 100% (P-35); QF scholarships aren't measured by this sheet (D-031).

### 7. More graduates every year
- The six universities with graduates in every class (CMU-Q, GU-Q, NU-Q, TAMU-Q, WCM-Q, HEC-Q) went from **414 graduates (class of 2019–20) to 645 (class of 2024–25): up 56%**.
- With HBKU, the class of 2023–24 had **965 graduates** across eight universities.

**See it:** 4 · Graduating, *Graduates per class*.

### 8. Most students finish, but on-time graduation varies widely
- Share of the latest entering class that graduated **on time**: **CMU-Q 81%** (entered 2020–21), HEC-Q 71% (2023–24, graduate programs), WCM-Q 64% (2021–22), **TAMU-Q 52%** (2020–21).
- Within **150% of normal time** (six years for a bachelor's): TAMU-Q 88%, WCM-Q 90% (both entered 2019–20), HEC-Q 99% (2022–23).

**See it:** 4 · Graduating, *Graduation rates by entering class*. **Keep in mind:** only these four universities report full rates; GU-Q, HBKU and NU-Q report graduates but not how many entered (P-25). Rates count only once their time has passed (D-032).

### 9. At TAMU-Q, Qatari students take longer to graduate; at CMU-Q there's no gap
- TAMU-Q, entering classes 2017–18 to 2020–21: **28–49% of Qatari students graduated on time, against 63–73% of Non-Qatari students**.
- **Within 150% of normal time the gap almost closes**: 82–88% of Qatari and 79–92% of Non-Qatari students graduated. Qatari engineering students mostly finish, but more of them need a fifth or sixth year.
- CMU-Q: Qatari **80–87%**, Non-Qatari 79–83% on time, no gap.
- WCM-Q shows a smaller gap in the same direction, on classes of 13–18 Qatari students.

**See it:** 4 · Graduating, choose TAMU-Q (or CMU-Q), then Students: *Qatari* and *Non-Qatari*. **Keep in mind:** classes of 39–68 students per nationality at TAMU-Q; it's a pattern across four classes, not one.

### 10. About three in four graduates are working a year later
- Class of 2023–24, six universities: **73% employed, 11% in further study, 9% still searching** a year after graduating.
- At the five universities with outcomes for every class since 2021–22 (CMU-Q, NU-Q, TAMU-Q, VCU-Q, WCM-Q), employment rose from **67% to 74%**; across all that reported, still searching fell from 19% to 9%.
- **Qatari graduates are more often employed (78% against 68%); Non-Qatari graduates more often go on to further study (17% against 3%).**

**See it:** 5 · After graduation. **Keep in mind:** rates are of the whole class, so graduates whose outcome isn't known count as neither (D-021); outcomes start with the class of 2021–22 for Education City (D-042).

### 11. Graduates stay in Qatar, except the doctors
- Of graduates employed a year later, **90% work in Qatar**: **100% of Qatari** and **81% of Non-Qatari** graduates (class of 2023–24).
- WCM-Q is the exception: all its graduates are employed, but only **46% in Qatar**. Its doctors mostly go abroad for residency.

**See it:** 5 · After graduation, *Working in Qatar* and *Each university*.

## What the data taught us about the data

Building the dashboard meant reading 60 files from nine universities. The same form is filled in quite differently:

- **The same question, different answers.** CMU-Q reports last year's graduates while most report this year's (D-011); TAMU-Q's "graduated within 150%" includes the on-time graduates while others' doesn't (D-027); TAMU-Q reports no drop-outs and counts leavers as non-completers (P-39); each university labels class year its own way (D-040).
- **Missing years.** CMU-Q has no data for 2021–22; VCU-Q's 2022–23 registered students weren't reported; HBKU appears only in some years; HEC-Q reports no outcomes; TAMU-Q's 2024–25 file covers one program (D-038).
- **Blanks that look like answers.** Totals that are formulas over empty cells read as 0 (P-13); a blank male count made VCU-Q look 100% female (P-40); "n/a" read as a real answer (D-040).
- **Who's included changes the total.** When HBKU joined the data in 2022–23, Education City's Qatari share fell from 49% to 40%, and its 21,252 applicants a year later outnumbered every other university's together. Coverage moves the totals more than any real change.

Thirteen automatic checks now catch these on every build, and 45 written decisions say how each is handled (`docs/DECISIONS.md`).

## Recommendations for the Student Data Collection

1. **Date every graduate count**: ask for graduates "who completed between [date] and [date]" instead of "this year".
2. **Define the terms on the form**: "within 150%" includes the on-time graduates; a drop-out is distinct from a non-completer.
3. **Separate zero from unknown**: no formula totals in the form; an explicit "not available" choice instead of a blank.
4. **Ask for the full nationality × gender split**, with a "fewer than 5" code instead of an empty cell.
5. **Use fixed answer lists** for class year, attendance and scholarship, instead of free text.
6. **Collect QF scholarship numbers** from QF Financial Services into the same dataset, since universities report them as 0.
7. **Check each submission when it arrives**, with checks like this project's thirteen (C01–C13), so questions go back to the university while the year is fresh.
8. **One file per university per year**, with corrections sent as a new version of that year, not filed under another year.

## Limits of these findings

- The data is aggregated counts, not individual students: it shows how groups differ, not why.
- Graduation rates come from four universities and outcomes from up to seven, at one year after graduation.
- Single-year changes (such as the fall in new Qatari students in 2024–25) need another year to tell a trend from noise.
